Open a Hindu calendar and every date comes with a short line of Sanskrit terms: *Shukla Ekadashi, Budhavara, Rohini, Siddha, Bava*. That line is the **Panchang** of the day. *Panch-anga* means "five limbs", and those five measurements, taken together, describe the character of a day in the traditional calendar.

<KeyTakeaway>
A Panchang records five things for each day: the <strong>Tithi</strong> (lunar day), <strong>Vara</strong> (weekday), <strong>Nakshatra</strong> (the Moon's lunar mansion), <strong>Yoga</strong> (a Sun–Moon combination) and <strong>Karana</strong> (half a Tithi). All five come from the real positions of the Sun and Moon, which is why their timings shift every day and from place to place.
</KeyTakeaway>

## 1. Tithi: the lunar day

The Tithi is the heart of the Hindu calendar. It measures how far the Moon has moved ahead of the Sun: every **12°** of separation is one Tithi, so a full cycle from new moon to new moon contains **30 Tithis**.

They are split into two fortnights, or *pakshas*:

- **Shukla Paksha**, the bright or waxing fortnight, from the new moon to the full moon.
- **Krishna Paksha**, the dark or waning fortnight, from the full moon back to the new moon.

| Tithi | Name | Tithi | Name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Pratipada | 9 | Navami |
| 2 | Dwitiya | 10 | Dashami |
| 3 | Tritiya | 11 | Ekadashi |
| 4 | Chaturthi | 12 | Dwadashi |
| 5 | Panchami | 13 | Trayodashi |
| 6 | Shashthi | 14 | Chaturdashi |
| 7 | Saptami | 15 | Purnima (full moon) or Amavasya (new moon) |
| 8 | Ashtami | | |

Because the Moon's speed varies, a Tithi can last anywhere from about 19 to 26 hours. Occasionally a Tithi begins and ends between two sunrises and is "skipped" in the calendar, or spans two sunrises and appears twice. This is why a festival sometimes seems to move by a day between calendars.

Most festivals are fixed by Tithi: Ganesh Chaturthi on Shukla Chaturthi of Bhadrapada, Janmashtami on Krishna Ashtami, Diwali on the new moon (Amavasya) that falls in Ashvin or Kartika, depending on the regional calendar. Ekadashi, the eleventh Tithi of each fortnight, is widely observed as a fasting day.

## 2. Vara: the weekday

The Vara is the familiar seven-day week, but in the Panchang each day runs **from sunrise to sunrise**, not midnight to midnight. Each Vara is named for a planet:

| Vara | Day | Planet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ravivara | Sunday | Sun |
| Somavara | Monday | Moon |
| Mangalavara | Tuesday | Mars |
| Budhavara | Wednesday | Mercury |
| Guruvara | Thursday | Jupiter |
| Shukravara | Friday | Venus |
| Shanivara | Saturday | Saturn |

The same planet-to-day sequence appears across many cultures, which is why "Saturday" and "Shanivara" both belong to Saturn.

## 3. Nakshatra: the Moon's mansion

The third limb is the [Nakshatra](/blog/what-is-a-nakshatra) the Moon is passing through, one of 27 segments of 13°20′. The Moon spends roughly a day in each. The day's Nakshatra matters most in **Muhurta**, the choice of auspicious times: many traditions prefer particular Nakshatras for weddings, housewarmings or starting a journey. Our [list of the 27 Nakshatras](/blog/27-nakshatras-list) gives each one's ruler and deity.

## 4. Yoga: the Sun–Moon combination

This Yoga is different from the planetary *yogas* in a birth chart. Here, the longitudes of the Sun and Moon are **added together**, and the total is divided into 27 segments of 13°20′. Each segment has a name:

Vishkambha, Priti, Ayushman, Saubhagya, Shobhana, Atiganda, Sukarma, Dhriti, Shoola, Ganda, Vriddhi, Dhruva, Vyaghata, Harshana, Vajra, Siddhi, Vyatipata, Variyan, Parigha, Shiva, Siddha, Sadhya, Shubha, Shukla, Brahma, Indra and Vaidhriti.

Tradition classes a few, such as Vyatipata and Vaidhriti, as inauspicious for beginning important work, and many others as favourable.

## 5. Karana: half a Tithi

A Karana is half a Tithi, or **6°** of Sun–Moon separation, so there are 60 in a lunar month. There are only 11 Karana names:

- **Seven movable Karanas** that repeat in sequence through the month: Bava, Balava, Kaulava, Taitila, Gara, Vanija and Vishti.
- **Four fixed Karanas** that occur once each around the new moon: Shakuni, Chatushpada, Naga and Kimstughna.

*Vishti*, also called *Bhadra*, is traditionally avoided for auspicious beginnings.

## Beyond the five limbs

A modern Panchang usually adds daily timings calculated from local sunrise and sunset:

- **Sunrise, sunset, moonrise and moonset.**
- **Rahu Kaal, Yamaganda and Gulika Kaal**: segments of the day traditionally avoided for new ventures.
- **Abhijit Muhurta**: the period around local noon, traditionally considered favourable.
- **Choghadiya**: eight daytime and eight night-time periods, popular in western India.

Our guide to [Rahu Kaal and Abhijit Muhurta](/blog/rahu-kaal-abhijit-muhurta-explained) explains how these are worked out.

## Amanta and Purnimanta: two ways to count a month

Indian calendars also differ in where a month ends. In the **Amanta** system, followed widely in south and west India, a month ends at the new moon. In the **Purnimanta** system, common in north India, it ends at the full moon. The Tithis are identical, but the month name attached to the dark fortnight differs. That is why some festivals appear to fall in different months in different regional calendars even though they're celebrated on the same day.

## How people use a Panchang

- **Festivals and fasts.** Knowing the Tithi tells you when Ekadashi, Purnima or a festival falls.
- **Choosing a date.** Families consult the Panchang, often with a priest or astrologer, when setting a date for a wedding, a [Griha Pravesh](/blog/griha-pravesh-puja) or another ceremony.
- **Planning the day.** Some people simply check Rahu Kaal before starting something new.

The Panchang is a traditional framework for marking time; how much weight to give it is a personal and family choice. Its astronomy is exact, and its judgements of "auspicious" and "inauspicious" come from the tradition.
