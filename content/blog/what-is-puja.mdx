In millions of Indian homes, the day begins the same way: someone lights a lamp before a small shrine, offers a flower, rings a bell, and says a prayer. That is a **puja**, the everyday heart of Hindu worship. The same word also describes a festival celebration that draws a whole neighbourhood, a housewarming led by a priest and a temple ritual performed several times a day.

<KeyTakeaway>
<strong>Puja</strong> is the Hindu practice of worship in which a deity is honoured with offerings (water, flowers, incense, light and food) together with prayer, mantras or song. It can be as simple as lighting a lamp at home or as elaborate as a day-long ceremony, and its practice varies by region, community and family.
</KeyTakeaway>

## What the word means

*Pūjā* means honour, reverence or worship. The ritual it names is about **relationship**: the worshipper approaches the divine with care, offers what they can, and receives blessing in return. That blessing is often symbolised by *prasad*, the offered food shared afterwards.

## The deity as honoured guest

One helpful way to understand puja is through Indian hospitality. Many of the offerings follow what you would do for a revered guest arriving at your home. You'd welcome them and offer a seat, water to wash their feet and hands, a drink, a bath, fresh clothes, fragrance, flowers, light, and a meal, and then see them off with respect.

A traditional puja follows much the same sequence. Each step is called an *upachara*, an offering or act of service.

## The steps of a puja

Two sequences are common. The short one, **Panchopachara** (five offerings), is enough for daily worship:

| Offering | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Gandha** | Sandalwood paste or kumkum |
| **Pushpa** | Flowers |
| **Dhupa** | Incense |
| **Deepa** | A lit lamp |
| **Naivedya** | Food offered to the deity |

The fuller **Shodashopachara** has sixteen steps. Lists differ slightly between traditions, but a common version is:

| # | Upachara | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Avahana | Inviting the deity's presence |
| 2 | Asana | Offering a seat |
| 3 | Padya | Water to wash the feet |
| 4 | Arghya | Water for the hands |
| 5 | Achamana | Water to sip |
| 6 | Snana | Bathing the image |
| 7 | Vastra | Offering clothing |
| 8 | Yajnopavita | Sacred thread, or ornaments |
| 9 | Gandha | Sandalwood paste |
| 10 | Pushpa | Flowers |
| 11 | Dhupa | Incense |
| 12 | Deepa | Lamp |
| 13 | Naivedya | Food |
| 14 | Tambula | Betel leaf and nut |
| 15 | Pradakshina and namaskara | Circling and bowing |
| 16 | Pushpanjali or visarjana | A final offering of flowers, or bidding farewell |

Most ceremonies also open with a **sankalpa**, a formal statement of who is performing the puja, where, when and why, and begin by worshipping **Ganesha**, the remover of obstacles. Many end with **aarti**, the waving of a lamp with song, and a prayer asking forgiveness for any mistakes made. Our guide to [mantras, shlokas and aartis](/blog/mantra-shloka-stotra-aarti-difference) explains what is recited along the way.

## The main kinds of puja

| Kind | When | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Daily** (*nitya*) | Every day, often morning and evening | Lighting a lamp at the home shrine |
| **Occasional** (*naimittika*) | Festivals and life events | Diwali Lakshmi Puja, a [Griha Pravesh](/blog/griha-pravesh-puja), a child's naming ceremony |
| **Vow-based** (*kamya*, *vrata*) | Undertaken for a purpose or a vow | Satyanarayan Katha, a fast with puja |
| **Temple** | Performed by temple priests on a fixed schedule | Morning and evening worship in a temple |
| **Community** (*sarvajanik*) | Public celebrations | Durga Puja pandals, Ganeshotsav |

Life's milestones have their own ceremonies, the *samskaras*; see [pujas for life milestones](/blog/samskaras-life-milestone-pujas). New beginnings, from a new home to a new vehicle, have theirs too: see [Vahan puja](/blog/vahan-puja-new-vehicle) and [business opening pujas](/blog/business-opening-puja).

## Puja, havan and yajna

- A **puja** centres on an image, idol or symbol of the deity.
- A **havan** or *homa* centres on a consecrated fire, into which offerings of ghee, grains and herbs are made with mantras.
- A **yajna** is the broader Vedic term for sacrifice or offering, of which the fire ritual is the classic form.

Larger ceremonies often combine puja and havan. [Vastu Shanti](/blog/vastu-shanti-puja) is a typical example.

## Who performs a puja

Anyone can perform a simple puja at home, and in many families it's done by whoever is up first. Larger ceremonies, particularly those with Vedic mantras, a fire ritual or a precise **muhurat** (auspicious time), are usually led by a **pandit** or **purohit**, a priest trained in the procedure. Families often have a priest they have known for years; others find one through a temple or a service. Our guide on [booking a pandit for a puja at home](/blog/how-to-book-pandit-for-puja-at-home) covers what to ask.

## How practice varies

There is no single correct way to perform puja. Practice differs by:

- **Region:** Bengal's Durga Puja, Maharashtra's Ganeshotsav, Tamil and Kerala temple traditions and Gujarat's Navratri each have their own forms.
- **Tradition:** Vaishnava, Shaiva, Shakta and Smarta households emphasise different deities and procedures.
- **Family:** many families worship a *kuladevata* (family deity) with customs handed down for generations.

When general guidance and your family's custom differ, most priests would advise following your family.

## What puja means to people

Puja is a devotional practice, and what it brings is a matter of faith. Many people also describe more universal things: a quiet moment at the start of the day, a sense of gratitude, a way to mark important moments, and a thread connecting generations of a family. Those are reasons enough for many households, whatever their degree of belief.
