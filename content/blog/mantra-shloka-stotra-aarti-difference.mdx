Listen to any puja and you'll hear several kinds of recitation: a few syllables repeated on a mala, a verse chanted in Sanskrit, a long hymn of praise, a song sung while a lamp is waved. People often call all of them "mantras", but the tradition distinguishes between them, and knowing the difference makes a puja easier to follow.

<KeyTakeaway>
A <strong>mantra</strong> is a sacred formula recited in worship or meditation. A <strong>shloka</strong> is a verse. A <strong>stotra</strong> is a hymn of praise made of many verses. An <strong>aarti</strong> is the waving of a lamp and the song sung with it. A <strong>bhajan</strong> is a devotional song, and a <strong>chalisa</strong> is a forty-verse hymn.
</KeyTakeaway>

## At a glance

| Form | What it is | Typical language | Well-known example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mantra** | A sacred formula for worship or meditation, often repeated | Sanskrit, often Vedic | Gayatri mantra; "Om Namah Shivaya" |
| **Shloka** | A verse, often in the two-line *anushtubh* metre | Sanskrit | A verse of the Bhagavad Gita |
| **Stotra** | A hymn of praise of many verses | Sanskrit | Vishnu Sahasranama |
| **Aarti** | Waving a lamp, and the song sung with it | Hindi and regional languages | "Om Jai Jagdish Hare" |
| **Bhajan / kirtan** | A devotional song, often sung together | Regional languages | Countless, across every region |
| **Chalisa** | A hymn of forty verses | Awadhi, Hindi | Hanuman Chalisa |

## Mantra

A mantra is a word, syllable or formula recited for its sound and meaning. The oldest come from the Vedas and are traditionally chanted with precise pronunciation and pitch. The **Gayatri mantra**, from the Rigveda, is among the best known; so is the single syllable **Om**. Others are short devotional formulas addressed to a deity, such as "Om Namah Shivaya" or "Om Namo Narayanaya".

Mantras are often repeated a set number of times, a practice called **japa**, counted on a **mala** of 108 beads. The number 108 recurs across Hindu tradition; astrologers note, for instance, that there are 108 Nakshatra padas. Some mantras, particularly Vedic ones, are traditionally learned from a teacher. Your family or priest can guide you on which are appropriate for daily recitation.

## Shloka

*Shloka* simply means verse. Most commonly it refers to a Sanskrit couplet in the *anushtubh* metre, the metre of most of the Bhagavad Gita, the Mahabharata and the Ramayana. A shloka can be about anything: philosophy, story, instruction.

In daily worship, people often recite short devotional shlokas, like the verse to Ganesha that begins *Vakratunda Mahakaya* before starting any task, or verses of the Gita. When a shloka is recited as a prayer, it functions much like a mantra, which is why the words overlap in everyday speech.

## Stotra

A stotra is a **hymn of praise**: a longer composition of many verses addressed to a deity, often listing their names and qualities. The **Vishnu Sahasranama** (the thousand names of Vishnu), the **Lalita Sahasranama** and many hymns attributed to Adi Shankaracharya are well-known stotras. Reciting a stotra is a common part of weekly or festival worship.

## Aarti

*Aarti* names two things at once: the **ritual** of waving a lit lamp, usually in clockwise circles, before a deity, and the **song** sung while doing it. It is often the climax of a puja, accompanied by bells and clapping. Afterwards, those present pass their palms over the flame and touch them to their eyes or forehead, receiving the blessing of the light.

"**Om Jai Jagdish Hare**", composed in the nineteenth century, is sung in homes and temples across north India. Each deity has its own popular aartis, and regional traditions have their own.

## Bhajan, kirtan and chalisa

**Bhajans** and **kirtans** are devotional songs, often sung in groups, in every Indian language. They're part of the *bhakti*, or devotion, strand of Hindu practice, where love for the deity matters more than ritual precision.

A **chalisa** is a devotional hymn of forty verses (*chalis* means forty). The most famous is the **Hanuman Chalisa**, composed in Awadhi by the poet-saint Tulsidas, recited by millions, often on Tuesdays and Saturdays.

## Where each fits in a puja

| Point in the puja | What's usually recited |
| --- | --- |
| Beginning | A shloka or mantra to Ganesha |
| Sankalpa | A formal Sanskrit statement (by the pandit) or your own words |
| Invocation and offerings | Mantras for each offering, in a formal puja |
| Main worship | Mantras on a mala, a stotra, or a chalisa |
| Closing | Aarti, then a prayer asking forgiveness for any mistakes |
| Anytime | Bhajans |

## Getting started

- **Start small.** One mantra or shloka you understand is better than a long text you rush.
- **Learn by listening.** Pronunciation is easier to pick up from a recording or an elder than from text.
- **Know the meaning.** Most mantras and shlokas have widely available translations, and understanding them changes how they feel to recite.
- **Follow your tradition.** Families and communities have their own favourite prayers; those are a natural place to begin.
